A *chart of accounts* — often shortened to **CoA** — sounds like something only an accountant
needs to worry about. What it actually is, is simple: **a list of the pockets you sort every
bit of money into, coming in and going out.**

Without one, your bookkeeping ends up as a single large pile labelled "expenses". You know that
Rp 40 million went out this month, but not what it went on — so there is nothing you can act on.

This guide sets out the minimum list of accounts that is enough for a service business like a
workshop, salon, print shop, or laundry. You can copy it straight into a notebook or a
spreadsheet.

## The principle: few and actually used, not many and abandoned

The most common mistake when setting up a chart of accounts for the first time is making it too
detailed. Eighty lines get created, and two weeks later nobody can fill them in correctly.

The rule I would suggest: **if an account will not change a decision you make, do not create
it.** Splitting "electricity" from "water" is only useful if you are genuinely going to act on
them separately. If not, just merge them into "electricity & water".

Start small. Adding an account later is easy; untangling 80 accounts that have already been
filled in wrongly is a great deal more work.

## The five big groups

Every account belongs to one of these five groups. The numbering follows common convention, so
anyone who picks it up later can follow along:

| Code | Group | What it means in plain language |
|---|---|---|
| 1-xxxx | **Assets** | What you own |
| 2-xxxx | **Liabilities** | What you owe |
| 3-xxxx | **Equity** | The owner's share |
| 4-xxxx | **Income** | Money coming in from selling |
| 5-xxxx & 6-xxxx | **Expenses** | Money going out to run the business |

## A minimum chart of accounts for a service business

Here is the layout I think is enough to get going. Drop anything that does not apply to your
business.

### 1-xxxx — Assets

| Code | Account name | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 1-1010 | Cash | Notes and coins in the drawer |
| 1-1020 | Bank | The balance in the business account |
| 1-1200 | Accounts receivable | Customers you have served who have not paid yet |
| 1-1300 | Materials inventory | Materials and spare parts not yet used |
| 1-1500 | Equipment | Tools, computers, machines |
| 1-1600 | Accumulated depreciation | The drop in value of your equipment (see the note below) |

### 2-xxxx — Liabilities

| Code | Account name | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 2-1010 | Accounts payable | Supplier bills you have not paid yet |
| 2-1200 | Wages & commission payable | Pay your staff have earned but not yet received |
| 2-1300 | Tax payable | Tax not yet remitted |
| 2-2000 | Bank loan | What is left on the loan |

### 3-xxxx — Equity

| Code | Account name | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 3-1010 | Owner's capital | Money you have put into the business |
| 3-1020 | Owner's drawings | Business money you have taken out for personal use |
| 3-2000 | Retained earnings | Profit from earlier years that was left in |

> **The drawings account matters a lot.** It is where you record money taken out of the
> business for personal use. For as long as that goes unrecorded, your profit will always look
> bigger than it really is.

### 4-xxxx — Income

| Code | Account name | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 4-1000 | Service income | What the service work brings in |
| 4-2000 | Product sales | Spare parts or products sold |
| 4-9000 | Sales discounts | Discounts you have given |

Keeping service income separate from product sales is very useful for a workshop or salon,
because the margins on the two are usually a long way apart.

### 5-xxxx — Cost of goods sold

These are the costs that **attach directly** to the work you sell.

| Code | Account name | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 5-1000 | Materials used | Consumables used on the work |
| 5-2000 | Cost of products sold | What you paid for the parts that sold |
| 5-3000 | Wages & commission on jobs | Commission for the mechanic or therapist doing the work |

### 6-xxxx — Operating expenses

Costs that keep running whether there is work or not.

| Code | Account name | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| 6-1000 | Non-production staff wages | Cashier, admin |
| 6-2000 | Rent | |
| 6-2100 | Electricity, water & internet | |
| 6-3000 | Supplies & stationery | |
| 6-4000 | Marketing & advertising | |
| 6-5000 | Maintenance & repairs | |
| 6-6000 | Equipment depreciation | |
| 6-9000 | Other expenses | A temporary home, see the note below |

## Why the cost of sales is kept apart from operating expenses

This is the split people most often collapse into one, even though the split is exactly what
makes the report useful.

Keeping them apart lets you see two different things:

- **Gross profit** = Income − Cost of goods sold (group 5). Answers: *is my selling price
  reasonable?*
- **Net profit** = Gross profit − Operating expenses (group 6). Answers: *can this business pay
  for itself?*

If every cost is lumped together, neither question can be answered on its own — and you cannot
tell whether the trouble is in your pricing or in your running costs.

## Depreciation, briefly

Equipment like a compressor or a printing press loses value as it gets used. That loss of value
is called **depreciation**, and it is worth recording so that your profit does not look bigger
than it is.

The simplest method: divide the purchase price evenly across the years you expect to use it.

For example, a machine bought for Rp 24,000,000 with an expected working life of 4 years (48
months) works out to about Rp 500,000 of depreciation a month.

If this part feels confusing at the start, **skip it for now**. It is better to record 90% of
things correctly and consistently than to stop altogether because you tripped over depreciation
in month one.

## How to start using it

1. **Copy the list above** into a notebook or spreadsheet. Delete what does not apply.
2. **Stick it up near the till.** Whoever does the recording should be able to see it without
   having to ask.
3. **Record every transaction with its account code.** It feels slow at first; after two weeks
   it becomes habit.
4. **At month end, total up each account.** From there a profit and loss statement almost
   builds itself.
5. **Review every 3 months.** An account nobody ever fills? Delete it. One with a mix of things
   in it? Split it.

What decides whether this works is not how tidy the list is, but **how consistently it gets
filled in**. A simple chart of accounts used every day is far more useful than a complete one
filled in once and then left alone.

Later on, when there are too many transactions to record by hand, that is when a system that
posts the entries for you starts to make sense. But the order stays the same as in the earlier
guide: **understand the grouping first, then look for a tool.** A system only speeds up work
you already understand — it cannot stand in for that understanding.
