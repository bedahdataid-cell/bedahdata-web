"We did 80 million in revenue this month." That sentence feels like a relief. But revenue is
the number most likely to leave a business owner calm when they should be paying attention —
because nothing has been subtracted from it yet.

These three numbers get swapped around in everyday conversation, even though each one answers a
different question. Mix them up, and the decisions you make get mixed up along with them.

## Revenue: how much came in from selling

**Revenue** (also called sales, or turnover) is the total value of the goods or services you
sold in a period. Nothing has been taken off it yet.

If your workshop took in payments for services and spare parts worth Rp 80,000,000 in a month,
then your revenue for that month is Rp 80,000,000.

One thing worth watching: **revenue is not the same as the cash in your drawer.** If a customer
took the goods but has not paid yet, the value still counts as revenue — even though you are
not holding the money. This is exactly why a business can have big revenue and still be short
of cash.

> Revenue answers: **"How much did I manage to sell?"**
> Not: "How much did I get to keep?"

## Gross profit: what is left after the cost of what you sold

**Gross profit** is revenue minus your **cost of goods sold (COGS)** — the costs that attach
directly to the thing you sold.

```
Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of goods sold
```

For a shop: what you paid for the goods that actually sold. For a service business: the
materials used and the pay of whoever did the work.

Let us carry on with the workshop. Every number below is **an example to explain the idea**,
not anybody's real figures:

| Item | Example figure |
|---|---|
| Revenue (services + spare parts) | Rp 80,000,000 |
| Purchase cost of the parts sold | Rp 38,000,000 |
| Mechanics' commission | Rp 12,000,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **Rp 30,000,000** |

Gross profit is what has to cover every other cost you have. If it is thin, then no matter how
big the revenue is, the business will feel like heavy going.

> Gross profit answers: **"Out of each sale, how much is left to run the business with?"**

## Net profit: what genuinely remains

**Net profit** is gross profit minus all your operating costs — the costs that keep running
even when nothing is being sold.

```
Net profit = Gross profit − Operating costs
```

Operating costs include rent, electricity and water, wages for staff who are not doing the work
itself (cashier, admin), internet, equipment upkeep, and so on.

Continuing the same example:

| Item | Example figure |
|---|---|
| Gross profit | Rp 30,000,000 |
| Rent | Rp 6,000,000 |
| Admin & cashier wages | Rp 7,000,000 |
| Electricity, water, internet | Rp 2,500,000 |
| Equipment upkeep & other | Rp 3,500,000 |
| **Net profit** | **Rp 11,000,000** |

Out of Rp 80 million in revenue, what genuinely remains is Rp 11 million — about **13.75%** in
this example.

That is the number that actually answers "how much is my business making". And it is also the
number small business owners are least likely to know, because getting to it takes reasonably
disciplined record-keeping.

## Why the three have to be kept apart

Picture the decisions below, and notice which number each one really needs.

**Decision 1: "Revenue is down 10%, I need a big promotion."**
If the promotion costs more than the gross profit it brings in, revenue does go up — but net
profit goes down. Busy is not the same as profitable.

**Decision 2: "I want to raise my staff's wages."**
The relevant number is not revenue, it is net profit. Wages come out of that, not out of the
money coming in.

**Decision 3: "Product A is flying off the shelf, I should stock more of it."**
Check the gross profit first. A fast seller on a thin margin can bring in less than a slow
seller on a fat one.

## The three mistakes that come up most

**1. Treating the cash in the drawer as profit.** That money is still mixed: some of it is
capital, some is owed to suppliers, and some already belongs to your staff as commission.

**2. Forgetting to pay yourself.** If you are in there working every day, your labour has a
value. A business that is only "profitable" because the owner takes no wage is covering a loss
with their own time.

**3. Mixing personal money with business money.** While the two are tangled together, none of
these three numbers can be worked out correctly. If you are only going to tidy up one thing,
start here — it has the biggest effect and it costs nothing.

## A realistic way to begin

You do not need a full set of financial statements to start getting something out of this:

1. **Separate the business account from your personal one.** The cheapest step, and the one
   that changes the most.
2. **Record daily revenue.** One number a day is enough.
3. **Record the cost of each item or service sold.** For goods, that is simply what you paid
   for them.
4. **Gather your monthly fixed costs.** These rarely change, so you only have to set them out
   once.
5. **Work out all three at the end of the month.** An hour is enough for your first month.

After three months you will start seeing patterns — and patterns are what let you decide on
something other than a hunch.

Let us be straight about it: knowing these three numbers **does not automatically make your
business more profitable**. What changes is that you know where the problem sits. The rest is
still your decision, and that is how it should be.
