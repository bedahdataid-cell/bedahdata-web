If you sell products, working out the cost is fairly easy: you buy for Rp 10,000, you sell for
Rp 15,000, your gross profit is Rp 5,000. The number is printed right there on the purchase
receipt.

Services do not work like that. No receipt ever says "cost of one motorbike service". So a lot
of workshop and salon owners set their prices the way that makes the most sense at the time:
they look at what the shop down the road charges, then adjust a little.

That can carry you for years. The trouble only shows up when you ask yourself: **out of all the
services I sell, which one is actually making me the most money?** Without a cost figure, that
question cannot be answered — only guessed at.

This guide walks through how to work it out. You can do all of it with paper and a calculator,
without buying anything.

## Why the cost of a service is so often miscalculated

There are two mistakes I run into most often, and both of them look perfectly reasonable.

**First: counting only the materials.** A salon owner works out the cost of a hair colouring
service from the dye that gets used, and nothing else. But the therapist's time is in there too
— an hour and a half of it — and that time has a cost.

**Second: throwing every cost in at once.** The opposite mistake. Someone tries to load the
rent, the electricity, the cashier's wages, and everything else onto every single service. The
result is a calculation so complicated it never gets finished, and eventually gets abandoned.

The middle path: split your costs into **costs that stick to the job** and **costs that keep
running whether there is a job or not**.

## The three parts of a service cost

### 1. Materials that are genuinely used up

This is the easiest part, and also the part most often estimated rather than measured.

For a salon: how many grams of dye go on one head, not the price of a whole tub. For a
workshop: the oil, the brake pads, or the spare parts fitted during one service.

> **The practical way to do it:** if one tub of hair dye costs Rp 120,000 and covers roughly 6
> heads, then your material cost per service is about Rp 20,000. You do not need to be accurate
> down to the gram — what matters is that you stop guessing from nothing.

### 2. The pay of whoever does the work

This is the part most often left out in a service business, even though it is usually the
biggest one.

If your therapist is paid a 30% commission on the value of the service, you can use that figure
straight away. But if they are on a fixed monthly wage, you need to turn it into an hourly cost:

```
Cost per hour = Monthly wage ÷ Hours worked in a month
```

An example calculation: a wage of Rp 3,000,000 a month, working 25 days × 8 hours = 200 hours.
That comes to roughly Rp 15,000 per hour. If one service takes 1.5 hours, the labour that
sticks to that service is about Rp 22,500.

**The numbers above are only an illustration of the method**, not market data and not a
recommendation about what to pay anyone. Swap in the numbers from your own business.

</div>

### 3. Fixed costs that still need covering

Rent, electricity, water, internet, the cashier's wages — all of these keep running even on a
quiet day. They do not stick to any one job, but they still have to be covered by the services
you sell.

The simplest way to handle this in a small business:

```
Fixed cost per job = Total monthly fixed costs ÷ Number of jobs in a month
```

Say, for example, your fixed costs are Rp 8,000,000 a month and you handle an average of 400
jobs. Then each job needs to carry about Rp 20,000.

That "number of jobs in a month" figure matters enormously — and this is where a lot of
businesses come to a halt, because nobody has been writing it down. If you do not have it yet,
start counting today: one column in a notebook, one line per job.

## Pulling it into a single number

Let us put it together using a **hair colouring service** as the example. Once again, every
number below is **an example to show the method**, not a price you should be charging.

| Part | Example figure |
|---|---|
| Materials (dye used) | Rp 20,000 |
| Therapist's pay (1.5 hours) | Rp 22,500 |
| Fixed cost per job | Rp 20,000 |
| **Cost of the service** | **Rp 62,500** |

If you sell that service for Rp 150,000, then:

- Profit per service: Rp 150,000 − Rp 62,500 = **Rp 87,500**
- Margin: Rp 87,500 ÷ Rp 150,000 = **around 58%**

Now do the same for your other services. This is usually where the surprise turns up: the
service that gets booked the most is often not the one that earns the most, because it eats up
the most of your staff's time.

## What changes once you know the number

Let me be straight with you: **knowing your cost figure does not make your profit go up by
itself.** What changes is the quality of the decisions you are able to make.

Once you have the number, you can answer questions that used to be pure guesswork:

- Which services are worth pushing in a promotion, and which ones are better left quiet?
- Somebody asks for a 20% discount — does that still work or not?
- How many jobs a day do you need at minimum before your fixed costs are covered?
- If you raise the therapists' commission, where does that come from?

All of those decisions still belong to you. Only one thing changes: you make them while looking
at a number, instead of while guessing.

## Where to start if you have nothing written down

If you have no records at all right now, do not start by costing every service at once — that
usually stalls halfway.

1. **Pick the 3 services people book most often.** Three is enough to begin with.
2. **Write down the materials used over one week** for those three.
3. **Note how long each one takes on average.** A rough estimate is plenty to start.
4. **Add up your monthly fixed costs.** This is usually the quickest part, because the numbers
   barely move.
5. **Lay out the table** like the example above.

One week of writing things down already gives you a far better picture than years of guessing.

Later on, when you have many more services and doing this by hand starts eating your time, that
is the point where a system that calculates it for you starts to make sense. But the order
matters: **understand the method first, then look for a tool.** Do it the other way round and
all you get is a report you do not understand.
